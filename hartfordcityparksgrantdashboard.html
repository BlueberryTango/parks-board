How to read this — and what it isn't

This is not every grant that exists. There are thousands of private foundations alone. What's here is the set that realistically fits a non-entitlement Indiana city of ~5,900 with a parks department and a documented ADA problem. Treat it as a working file, not a finished census.

Verify before you rely. Deadlines move, programs get defunded, and staff turn over. Each sheet carries a "verified" date. Anything older than 90 days shows in red — re-check it against the agency's own page before you build a plan around it.

The metric people forget: reimbursement vs. upfront. Several of these pay you back only after you've spent the money. A 20%-match grant that reimburses still requires the city to front 100% of the cash. That's a council conversation, not a parks conversation, and it kills more small-town projects than scoring ever does.

Fit score is a weighted composite of six factors, shown in full on each sheet. It's a sorting aid and a conversation starter, not a verdict. A 45 you can actually win beats an 80 you can't fund the match on.

Don't trust grant aggregator sites. The T-Mobile sheet is in here as a live example: the program concluded permanently in June 2026, and third-party databases were still advertising it as open weeks later. Confirm every opportunity on the funder's own website before spending time on it.

The 501(c)(3) pattern. Several strong funders — Finish Line among them — will only accept nonprofit applicants. A Friends of Hartford City Parks group is the key to that whole category, and it is the one structural change that raises the ceiling on everything else here.
